Module 2 Overview

Why This Module Matters

Module 2 is about exploratory data analysis (EDA): looking carefully at data before drawing conclusions. Whether the data describe gas prices, material strengths, or wait times, the goal is the same — understand what the numbers look like, summarize them honestly, and see how variables are related. The topics in this module matter because the wrong summary or the wrong plot can quietly mislead you, while the right one can turn a table of numbers into a clear story. In this module, that table of numbers is real: a dataset of regular gas prices from every state and the District of Columbia, collected on a single day, together with each state’s population, gasoline tax, and refining capacity.

What You’ll Learn

By the end of this module, you will understand the difference between categorical variables and quantitative variables, and between continuous variables and discrete variables. You will learn to describe the distribution of a variable and to choose the appropriate measure of central tendency — the mean, the median, or the mode — for the shape of the data. You will learn the measures of variability: quartiles, the interquartile range (IQR), the range, the variance, the standard deviation, and the minimum and maximum, along with the 1.5 × IQR rule for flagging outliers. You will learn when to summarize a categorical variable with proportions. And you will learn which visualizations fit which variables — histograms, boxplots, bar charts, and scatterplots — and how to compute grouped numeric summaries such as the mean, median, and standard deviation within groups.

By the End of This Module, You Should Be Able To

After completing this module, you should be able to work with a quantitative or categorical dataset and do several things with confidence:

  • Identify categorical and quantitative variables, and tell continuous variables from discrete ones.
  • Describe the distribution of a variable and recognize when it is skewed.
  • Choose and compute the appropriate measure of central tendency — the mean, the median, or the mode — for the shape of the data.
  • Compute and interpret quartiles, the interquartile range (IQR), the range, the variance, the standard deviation, and the minimum and maximum.
  • Use the 1.5 × IQR rule to identify outliers.
  • Summarize categorical variables with proportions.
  • Choose the right plot — a histogram, a boxplot, a bar chart, or a scatterplot — for the variables at hand.
  • Compute grouped numeric summaries (such as the mean, median, and standard deviation) within groups.
