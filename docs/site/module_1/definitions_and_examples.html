Glossary

Welcome to the glossary for Module 1. Each entry gives the definition of a key term, the sources used for that definition, and three examples drawn from a variety of fields to show the term in action. The terms are listed alphabetically.

Association

Definition: A relationship between two variables where changes in one are linked to changes in the other.

Example 1: A health survey finds that people who sleep more also report lower stress; the two are associated, but sleep does not necessarily cause the lower stress.

Example 2: Economic data show that consumer confidence and spending tend to rise and fall together, an association between two indicators.

Example 3: In a dataset of basketball players, height and rebounds per game are associated, even though height does not cause rebounds.

Causality

Definition: A cause-and-effect relationship where changing one variable produces a change in another. A relationship between two variables does not mean that one causes the other to occur; they may be related through a different variable.

Source: Introductory Statistics 2e, p. 32

Example 1: Raising the temperature of a chemical reaction reliably speeds it up, showing that temperature causes the faster reaction.

Example 2: A patient takes a blood-pressure medication and their readings drop every time, suggesting the drug causes the change.

Example 3: Increasing the voltage in a simple electrical circuit raises the current, a direct cause-and-effect relationship.

Cluster Sampling

Definition: In cluster sampling, the sampling unit contains groups of elements called clusters instead of individual elements of the population.

Source: Mathematical Statistics with Applications in R, p. 8 ; Introductory Statistics 2e, p. 55

Example 1: To study fifth-grade reading skills county-wide, a researcher randomly selects several schools and tests every fifth-grader in those schools.

Example 2: To survey city residents, urban planners randomly select a few city blocks and interview every household on the chosen blocks.

Example 3: To estimate fish populations in a lake, biologists randomly select several regions of the lake and count every fish in those regions.

Completely Randomized Design

Definition: The structure of the experiment in a completely randomized design is presumed to be such that the treatments are assigned to the experimental units completely at random.

Source: Mathematical Statistics with Applications in R, p. 349

Example 1: A farmer randomly assigns 40 identical plots of land to either fertilizer A or fertilizer B, then compares the crop yields.

Example 2: A psychology researcher randomly assigns 60 volunteers to either a new memory-training app or a crossword puzzle, then tests their recall after two weeks.

Example 3: A professor randomly assigns half of her class to an online textbook and half to a printed text, then compares final exam scores.

Confounding

Definition: A situation where the effects of multiple factors on a response cannot be separated, making it hard to tell which factor caused the result. Confounding makes it difficult or impossible to draw valid conclusions about the effect of each factor.

Source: Introductory Statistics 2e, p. 32

Example 1: A clinic tests a new diet pill, but the treatment group also receives weekly nutrition coaching, so the weight loss cannot be blamed on the pill alone.

Example 2: A study finds cleaner air near city parks, but those neighborhoods also have less traffic, so it is unclear which factor caused the improvement.

Example 3: A coffee chain opens drive-thrus only in busy suburbs and sales jump, but the jump might be due to the locations, not the drive-thru format.

Control

Definition: The factors that we could change but we deliberately keep fixed are called the controls in the experiment.

Source: Mathematical Statistics with Applications in R, p. 345

Example 1: A plant researcher grows every seedling in the same soil, light, and water so that fertilizer is the only difference.

Example 2: A car manufacturer keeps the same engine, tires, and driver for both fuel types being tested.

Example 3: A memory experiment gives both groups identical instructions and time limits so the study method is the only difference.

Control Group

Definition: The group in an experiment that receives an inactive treatment but is otherwise managed exactly as the other groups, so it serves as the standard comparison condition.

Source: Introductory Statistics 2e, p. 55

Example 1: In a drug trial, patients who receive a placebo serve as the control group against which the drug group is compared.

Example 2: In a fertilizer study, plants given only plain water form the control group.

Example 3: A school testing a new tutoring program gives one class the standard curriculum as the control group.

Convenience Sampling

Definition: A nonrandom method of selecting a sample that uses individuals who are easily accessible or readily available, and which may result in biased data.

Source: Introductory Statistics 2e, p. 55

Example 1: A mall kiosk surveys the first 20 shoppers who walk by because they are the easiest to approach.

Example 2: A hospital polls patients sitting in the waiting room during one morning because they are readily available.

Example 3: A student sends a survey link to friends in their own dorm, using whoever is nearby instead of a broader group.

Correlation

Definition: The correlation coefficient, r, is a numerical measure of the strength and direction of the linear association between two quantitative variables. Its value is always between -1 and 1.

Source: Introductory Statistics 2e, p. 642

Example 1: A materials engineer computes a correlation of 0.92 between alloy hardness and tensile strength, indicating a strong positive linear relationship.

Example 2: A cardiologist finds a correlation of -0.7 between daily exercise minutes and resting heart rate, meaning more exercise tends to come with a lower heart rate.

Example 3: A financial analyst finds a correlation near 0 between two stocks’ daily returns, so the stocks tend to move independently.

Descriptive Statistics

Definition: Methods of organizing, summarizing, and presenting data in the form of tables, graphs, and charts.

Source: Mathematical Statistics with Applications in R, p. 4

Example 1: A civil engineer records the load capacities of 30 bridge samples, then computes the average and a histogram to describe how strong the material typically is.

Example 2: A hospital quality team turns a year of patient wait times into a bar chart, a table of the most common complaints, and a median wait time to sum up clinic performance.

Example 3: A sports reporter summarizes a basketball team’s season by listing points per game, a histogram of game scores, and the win-loss record.

Experiment

Definition: A planned activity carried out under controlled conditions, in which the investigator deliberately applies a treatment to the units in an effort to observe a response.

Source: Introductory Statistics 2e, p. 212

Example 1: A medical researcher randomly assigns patients to receive a new drug or a placebo, then compares their recovery times.

Example 2: An engineer randomly assigns batches of a product to two cooling methods and measures the failure rates.

Example 3: A psychologist assigns participants to a sleep-deprived or well-rested condition and measures their reaction times.

Hypothesis

Definition: A statistical hypothesis is a statement concerning the probability distribution of a random variable or population parameters that are inherent in a probability distribution.

Source: Mathematical Statistics with Applications in R, p. 254 ; Introductory Statistics 2e, p. 498

Example 1: “Patients who receive the new vaccine will have fewer flu infections than those who do not” is a hypothesis that can be tested with data.

Example 2: An engineer hypothesizes that a new alloy will not crack at temperatures above -40°C.

Example 3: A researcher hypothesizes that students who exercise daily will have higher average exam scores than students who do not.

Inferential Statistics

Definition: The methods of drawing inferences and making decisions about the population using the sample.

Source: Mathematical Statistics with Applications in R, p. 4 ; Introductory Statistics 2e, p. 444

Example 1: Using a poll of 1,000 voters to estimate the percent of all voters who support a candidate.

Example 2: Testing 30 widgets from a factory line to estimate the defect rate of the entire production run.

Example 3: Using a sample of 200 patients to conclude that a drug lowers cholesterol for the broader patient population.

Multistage Sampling

Definition: Multiphase or multistage sampling involves collection of some information from the whole sample and additional information either at the same time or later from subsamples of the whole sample.

Source: Mathematical Statistics with Applications in R, p. 9

Example 1: A national census first surveys every household for basic questions about age and sex, then randomly selects a sample of states, then surveys households within those selected states about income and education.

Example 2: A study first surveys all school districts, then interviews schools within selected districts, then surveys students within the selected schools.

Example 3: A public health study first surveys all clinics in a city about patient age and region, then randomly selects a sample of clinics, then tests blood samples from a sample of patients within those selected clinics.

Non-Response Bias

Definition: A bias that occurs when selected individuals do not provide data or refuse to participate, so the collected responses may no longer be representative of the population.

Source: Introductory Statistics 2e, p. 32

Example 1: In a phone survey, if working adults rarely answer during the day, the results overrepresent people who are home.

Example 2: A clinic’s patient satisfaction survey gets few responses from younger patients, so the results skew toward older patients.

Example 3: If dissatisfied customers ignore a restaurant survey while happy customers respond, the satisfaction rating appears inflated.

Observational Study

Definition: A study in which the analyst is just an observer of the treatments on a sample of experimental units.

Source: Mathematical Statistics with Applications in R, p. 346

Example 1: Researchers record students’ sleep habits and exam scores without changing anyone’s routine.

Example 2: Astronomers observe how the brightness of a star changes over time without manipulating the star.

Example 3: Economists study the spending habits of people who already shop online without assigning anyone to shop online.

Parameter

Definition: A numerical characteristic of a population.

Source: Mathematical Statistics with Applications in R, p. 3 ; Introductory Statistics 2e, p. 55

Example 1: The true average blood pressure of all adults in a city is a parameter.

Example 2: The true proportion of all seeds in a batch that germinate is a parameter.

Example 3: The true unemployment rate for the entire country is a parameter.

Population

Definition: The collection or set of all objects or measurements that are of interest to the collector.

Source: Mathematical Statistics with Applications in R, p. 3 ; Introductory Statistics 2e, p. 55

Example 1: If a study focuses on all first-year engineering students at a university, those students are the population.

Example 2: All batteries produced in a factory this month are the population for a battery-life study.

Example 3: All the fish in a particular lake are the population for a fish-counting study.

Randomization

Definition: The process of assigning experimental units to treatment conditions in an entirely chance manner.

Source: Mathematical Statistics with Applications in R, p. 348

Example 1: Flipping a coin to decide whether each patient receives the drug or the placebo is randomization.

Example 2: A farmer uses a random number generator to decide which plots receive which fertilizer.

Example 3: A teacher shuffles index cards to assign students to study-method groups.

Replication

Definition: The same treatment is applied several times to the same experimental units or one time to several similar experimental units.

Source: Mathematical Statistics with Applications in R, p. 346

Example 1: A lab tests a new drug on 50 mice rather than 1 so the result can be trusted.

Example 2: An engineer runs a stress test on 10 identical bolts to confirm the design’s strength.

Example 3: A farmer plants the same seed variety in 30 plots to verify the yield is consistent.

Research Question

Definition: The question the study is designed to answer about a population or process.

Example 1: “Does the new turbine design vibrate less than the current one?” is a research question.

Example 2: “Can a daily walking program reduce average blood pressure in adults?” is a research question.

Example 3: “Does a flipped classroom improve exam scores compared with lectures?” is a research question.

Sample

Definition: A subset of data selected from a population.

Source: Mathematical Statistics with Applications in R, p. 3 ; Introductory Statistics 2e, p. 55

Example 1: The 1,000 voters interviewed in a poll are a sample of all voters.

Example 2: The 30 batteries tested are a sample of the entire month’s production.

Example 3: The 40 fish caught and measured are a sample of all the fish in the lake.

Sampling Bias

Definition: Bias in selection of elements of the sample.

Source: Mathematical Statistics with Applications in R, p. 9 ; Introductory Statistics 2e, p. 55

Example 1: Surveying only people in a university library when the study is about the whole town creates sampling bias.

Example 2: Testing only athletes when measuring fitness in the general population creates sampling bias.

Example 3: Surveying only online shoppers when the target population includes all consumers creates sampling bias.

Sampling Frame

Definition: The list of all of the population members from which a sample is selected.

Source: Mathematical Statistics with Applications in R, p. 6

Example 1: A university directory used to select students for a survey is a sampling frame.

Example 2: A voter registration list used to pick households for a poll is a sampling frame.

Example 3: A customer email list used to select recipients for a satisfaction survey is a sampling frame.

Self-Selection Bias

Definition: A type of sampling bias that occurs when people decide for themselves whether to become part of the sample.

Example 1: An online poll answered only by people who choose to click the link can have self-selection bias.

Example 2: A product review site that only collects ratings from customers who voluntarily rate items has self-selection bias.

Example 3: A wellness study that recruits volunteers may attract mainly people already interested in fitness, which biases the results.

Simple Random Sampling

Definition: A sample selected in such a way that every element of the population has an equal chance of being chosen.

Source: Mathematical Statistics with Applications in R, p. 6 ; Introductory Statistics 2e, p. 56

Example 1: Drawing six numbered balls from a well-mixed bin gives every number an equal chance of being selected.

Example 2: Using a random number generator to pick 25 students from a full class roster is simple random sampling.

Example 3: Numbering every box in a shipment and randomly selecting 10 to inspect is simple random sampling.

Statistic

Definition: A numerical characteristic of a sample.

Source: Mathematical Statistics with Applications in R, p. 4 ; Introductory Statistics 2e, p. 56

Example 1: The average age of the 1,000 surveyed voters is a statistic.

Example 2: The average failure load of the 10 tested bolts is a statistic.

Example 3: The average length of the 40 measured fish is a statistic.

Stratified Sampling

Definition: A sample obtained by stratifying the sampling frame based on some factor or factors and then selecting some elements from each of the strata.

Source: Mathematical Statistics with Applications in R, p. 7 ; Introductory Statistics 2e, p. 56

Example 1: A researcher splits students by grade level and randomly samples from each grade so every grade is represented.

Example 2: A health study divides the population by age group and samples within each group so older and younger adults are both covered.

Example 3: A company divides its customers by income level and surveys each group separately.

Systematic Sampling

Definition: A sample in which every Kth element in the sampling frame is selected after a suitable random start for the first element.

Source: Mathematical Statistics with Applications in R, p. 7 ; Introductory Statistics 2e, p. 56

Example 1: Choosing every 20th customer who enters a store for a survey is systematic sampling.

Example 2: Inspecting every 100th unit coming off an assembly line is systematic sampling.

Example 3: Sampling every 10th tree along a forest transect is systematic sampling.

Treatment

Definition: A treatment is a specific condition or intervention applied to the experimental units in an experiment. Different treatments correspond to different levels of a factor.

Source: Mathematical Statistics with Applications in R, p. 345 ; Introductory Statistics 2e, p. 56

Example 1: A new medication, a placebo, or a standard therapy can each be a treatment in a medical study.

Example 2: A particular fertilizer or watering schedule is a treatment in an agricultural experiment.

Example 3: A tutoring program or study app used in a classroom experiment is a treatment.

Undercoverage Bias

Definition: A type of sampling bias that occurs when some members of the target population have little or no chance of being selected.

Example 1: Sampling only from a library membership list when the goal is to study all city residents causes undercoverage bias.

Example 2: A survey conducted only online excludes residents without internet access, causing undercoverage bias.

Example 3: A phone survey that calls only landlines misses households with only cell phones, causing undercoverage bias.
