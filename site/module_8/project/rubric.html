Simple Linear Regression Mini-Project

Purpose

In this project, you will use simple linear regression to study the relationship between two quantitative variables from your assigned dataset. You will choose a predictor and a response, assess whether a linear model is reasonable, fit the model, interpret the slope and \(R^2\), construct a confidence interval for the slope, make a prediction with both a confidence interval for the mean response and a prediction interval for an individual response, and check the regression assumptions. The goal is to connect the regression methods from Module 8 with a complete analysis of your data.

Materials

You will receive:

  • A cleaned CSV dataset assigned to you.
  • A background description and data dictionary for that dataset.

Use these materials to describe the variables and the context of the data. Do not invent a source, population, collection date, or procedure that the supplied materials do not provide. If an important detail is not available, say so briefly.

You may use the Module 8 readings for review. You may also reuse the scatterplot, correlation, and other evidence from your exploratory data analysis mini-project. If you use the same predictor and response pair as before, you may refer to that earlier work instead of repeating it. If you choose a different pair, provide the new scatterplot, correlation, and interpretation.

What to do

1. Choose a predictor and a response

Select one quantitative predictor (independent) variable and one quantitative response (dependent) variable from your assigned dataset. You may use the same pair you used for the scatterplot in your exploratory data analysis mini-project, or you may choose a different pair.

If you choose a different pair, create a clearly labeled scatterplot, compute the correlation, and interpret the direction, form, and strength of the relationship. If you choose the same pair, refer to the earlier scatterplot and summarize what the correlation and relationship were. You do not need to repeat the scatterplot unless it helps your report.

Identify both variables by name and include units when they are available. Report the final sample size used in the regression after handling missing values.

2. Justify a simple linear regression model

Use the scatterplot to explain why a simple linear regression model is reasonable for these two variables. At this stage, focus only on the linearity assumption: does the average response appear to follow a straight-line pattern as the predictor changes?

If the relationship does not look linear, you may still fit the regression model, but say clearly that the linearity assumption is questionable and interpret the results with caution. Do not claim that the relationship is linear without evidence from the plot.

3. Fit the model and interpret the slope and \(R^2\)

Fit the simple linear regression model in R. Report the fitted equation using the variable names in context, and include the relevant R code and output.

Interpret the estimated slope in context. Your interpretation should state the expected change in the mean response associated with a one-unit increase in the predictor, including the correct units.

Report \(R^2\) and interpret it in context as a measure of model fit: the proportion of the variation in the response that is explained by the linear relationship with the predictor. Explain briefly what this value suggests about the fit, while remembering that a high \(R^2\) does not by itself prove that the model is appropriate.

4. Construct a 95% confidence interval for the slope

Construct a 95% confidence interval for the population slope \(\beta_1\). Report the estimate, standard error, degrees of freedom, critical value, margin of error, and endpoints, along with the relevant R code and output.

Interpret the interval in context, including the confidence level and units. Then use the interval to make a statement about significance. If the interval contains 0, then 0 is a plausible value for the slope, so the relationship is not statistically significant at the 5% level and it is possible that the true regression line is flat. If the interval is entirely above 0 or entirely below 0, state that the relationship is statistically significant and give the direction.

5. Make a prediction and construct response intervals

Choose a value of the predictor \(x\) at which you want to make a prediction. Choose a value within the observed range of the predictor and explain why the value is useful or interesting.

Report the predicted response at that value and interpret it in context. If your dataset contains an observed response at that same value of \(x\), report the residual for that observation and interpret whether the model overpredicted or underpredicted the response. If there is no observed response at that \(x\), state that a residual cannot be computed.

Construct a 95% confidence interval for the mean response at your chosen \(x\) and interpret it in context. Then construct a 95% prediction interval for an individual response at that same \(x\) and interpret it in context. Clearly distinguish the two intervals: the confidence interval is for the mean response of all units with that predictor value, while the prediction interval is for one individual response. Explain why the prediction interval is wider.

6. Check the regression assumptions

Check each of the four regression assumptions using appropriate plots and summaries. For each assumption, comment on whether the plot provides evidence of a violation.

  • Linearity: Use a residuals-versus-fitted-values plot, along with your earlier scatterplot, to look for curvature.
  • Independence: Use a residuals-versus-observation-order plot when order is meaningful, and consider how the data were collected. Describe any clustering, trends, or repeated measurements that could create dependence.
  • Constant variance: Use a residuals-versus-fitted-values plot to look for a fan shape or changing spread.
  • Normality: Use a normal Q-Q plot of the residuals to look for strong departures from normality, such as heavy tails or strong skew.

If you find a violation, explain what it is and what it means for your model or intervals. Do not simply list the assumptions or say that they are satisfied without evidence.

Report format and R code

Submit a readable report in one of these formats:

  • HTML: a rendered HTML report, for example from R Markdown or Quarto.
  • Word: a .docx document.
  • PDF: a .pdf document.

In every format, include the relevant R code alongside the output and written interpretations so your results can be followed. Use R functions and methods covered in the course materials. The Module 8 readings are available for review.

Submission checklist

  • The predictor and response are identified by name and units, and the final sample size is reported.
  • A new variable pair includes a clearly labeled scatterplot, a computed correlation, and an interpretation; a reused pair clearly references the earlier scatterplot and summarizes the correlation and relationship.
  • The scatterplot is used to justify whether a linear model is reasonable, and any nonlinearity is noted.
  • The regression model is fit correctly, the fitted equation is reported, the slope is interpreted in context, and \(R^2\) is reported and interpreted as a measure of model fit.
  • A 95% confidence interval for the slope is computed and interpreted in context, with a correct statement about significance.
  • A prediction is made at a chosen \(x\) value; the residual is reported and interpreted when an observed response exists at that \(x\).
  • A 95% confidence interval for the mean response and a 95% prediction interval for an individual response are computed and interpreted in context, and the two intervals are clearly distinguished.
  • All four regression assumptions are checked with appropriate plots, and any violations are discussed.
  • The report includes the relevant R code and output and is organized, readable, and complete.

General Expectations

  • Exploration beyond the required analysis is welcome, especially when the data raise interesting questions or reveal unexpected patterns.
  • Work should be organized, readable, and complete.
  • Use the assigned dataset and the supplied background and data dictionary; do not add unsupported details about the data’s source or collection.
  • If you reuse earlier exploratory work, reference it clearly rather than duplicating it.
  • Interpret results in terms of the variables and context in the project, not just as numbers.
  • Use the confidence interval for the slope to assess significance rather than relying on a separate hypothesis test.
  • Distinguish clearly between a confidence interval for the mean response and a prediction interval for an individual response.
  • Check all four regression assumptions with evidence rather than merely listing them.
  • Include the relevant R code with its output in the submitted report.
